What to engrave: choosing words that fit the space

How many characters fit on rings, pens, watches and boards, and how to choose an inscription you will still like in twenty years.

By the Personalized.Gifts editors. Updated November 19, 2026. How we research

In short

  • Count characters before you fall in love with a sentence: most rings hold 15 to 30, a knife handle about 20, a mug about 30.
  • A name and a date is a complete engraving. Everything after that is optional.
  • Write it the way you speak. Stock phrases age; your own words do not.

Engraving is the one kind of writing you cannot edit. That makes people freeze, then reach for a phrase they have seen on a hundred other gifts. There is a better way to decide, and it starts with a ruler, not a thesaurus.

Start with the space

Every object has a limit, and it is smaller than it looks. Makers publish their own numbers, and those always win, but the ranges below hold across most of what is sold.

ObjectTypical limitWhat fits
Ring, inside the band15 to 30 charactersA date and initials, or three or four words
Bar necklace10 to 15 charactersA name, a date, or coordinates on the back
Bracelet or cuff20 to 30 charactersA short sentence
Key ringTwo lines of about 14A name and a date
PenAbout 30 charactersA full name
Pocket knifeUnder 20 charactersA name and a year
Watch backThree lines of about 20A date, a line, your initial
Mug or glassAbout 30 acrossA name or a short line
Frame plateThree lines of about 25Names, date, place
Cutting boardTwo lines of about 28Family name and a year

Spaces and punctuation count as characters. Narrow bands and hard metals reduce the limit further, and resizing a ring later can damage what is written inside it.

The four things worth engraving

A name. The oldest reason to mark an object. Use the name they answer to. On anything used at work, the full name; on anything worn, the short one.

A date. Dates need no explanation and never embarrass anyone. Roman numerals make a date look like decoration to everyone except the person who knows what it marks.

A place. Coordinates do the same job as a date for a location: where you met, where they were born, the first front door.

A line. The hardest, and the one people remember. Keep it under six words. If it would sound odd said aloud across a kitchen table, it will read oddly in metal.

How to write a line that lasts

  • Use something one of you has actually said. A phrase from a message beats any quotation.
  • Describe the person, not the feeling. "First to laugh" will still be true in thirty years.
  • Leave out the age and the job. Both change.
  • Avoid published song lyrics and poems. They belong to someone else, and to everyone else who chose them.
  • For a pair of rings, split one sentence across the two.

Fonts, capitals and line breaks

Small engraving reads best in plain capitals. Script needs height to stay legible, so save it for watch backs, frames and boards. Break lines where you would pause if reading aloud, and never leave a single short word on the last line.

Before you approve the proof

Read it aloud, then read it backwards word by word; that is how proofreaders catch spelling. Check every digit of the date against a calendar, including the year. Ask the maker which side it goes on and which way up. Then stop editing. A plain, correct engraving is better than a clever one you are unsure of.

Questions people ask

Do spaces count towards the character limit?

Yes. Makers count every character, including spaces and punctuation. The engraving ideas tool counts the same way.

Is it better to engrave a name or a message?

A name, if you can only have one. It makes the object theirs and it is never wrong.

Can an engraving be removed or changed?

Rarely, and never invisibly. A jeweller can sometimes polish out a shallow engraving on a thick piece, but assume it is permanent.
